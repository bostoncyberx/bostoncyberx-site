## A. Slide Outline (14 slides, 30 minutes)

### Slide 1: Title
- Shadow AI: find it, govern it, keep the upside
- bostoncyberx · Transform with AI. Defend with AI. Achieve with AI.
- [Speaker name], [date]

**Speaker notes:** Welcome, one line on who you are. Set the contract: 30 minutes, then live Q&A. Promise the takeaways up front: a discovery walkthrough you can run yourself, a 5-part acceptable use policy, and a pattern that replaces bans. No fear pitch. This is a working session. (2 min)

### Slide 2: Two numbers that explain this session
- 82% of mid-market companies have AI in production
- Only 26% have it governed at scale
- About 55% of employees use AI tools nobody approved

**Speaker notes:** Read the numbers slowly. The 82/26 spread means most companies adopted before they governed. The 55% number is the mechanism: adoption is happening bottom-up, tool by tool, with no visibility. Ask for a show of hands in chat: "Type yes if you could list every AI tool used in your company this week." Expect silence. That silence is the session. (2 min)

### Slide 3: What shadow AI actually is
- Any AI tool used for work without approval or oversight
- Free chatbot accounts, browser extensions, AI features inside approved SaaS
- Personal accounts on personal devices, doing company work

**Speaker notes:** Define it without judgment. Include the sneaky category: AI features that switched on inside tools you already approved. The person using shadow AI is usually your best employee trying to go faster. Say that plainly. It sets up why bans fail. (2 min)

### Slide 4: Why this is a demand signal, not a discipline problem
- People adopt tools that make work faster
- The productivity is real
- Punishing it teaches hiding, not stopping

**Speaker notes:** Reframe before you talk risk. If you open with risk, the audience hears "another vendor selling fear." The honest read: your team ran a free pilot program for you and found the tools that work. Your job is to keep the upside and remove the exposure. (2 min)

### Slide 5: The exposure, precisely
- Client and company data leaving through unlogged channels
- Compliance obligations you can no longer answer honestly: 201 CMR 17.00, HIPAA, CMMC
- Insurance renewals and client questionnaires now ask about AI use

**Speaker notes:** Be specific and calm. No breach theater. The practical pain is provability: when the insurer or a client questionnaire asks how AI touches client data, "we don't know" is the worst answer available. Massachusetts firms carry 201 CMR 17.00 duties on resident data wherever it flows. (2 min)

### Slide 6: Find it. The discovery walkthrough
- Three passes: network, paper trail, people
- Runs in under two weeks with tools you already have
- Goal is a map, not a list of offenders

**Speaker notes:** Transition to the hands-on core. Emphasize: no new software purchase required for a first pass. The next three slides are the live walkthrough; share your screen or use annotated screenshots. (1 min)

### Slide 7: Pass 1: the network and identity layer
- DNS and web filter logs: hits on AI tool domains
- SSO and OAuth grants: which apps got connected to company accounts
- Approved-SaaS audit: which AI features are switched on

**Speaker notes:** Live walkthrough part 1. Show a real (sanitized) OAuth grant list and how third-party AI apps appear connected to email or drive accounts. This is the highest-signal, lowest-effort pass. Most rooms find surprises in OAuth grants alone. (3 min)

### Slide 8: Pass 2: the paper trail
- Expense reports: AI subscriptions on personal cards
- Browser extension inventories from device management
- Vendor invoices with AI add-on line items

**Speaker notes:** Live walkthrough part 2. Expenses are the underrated source: a $20 monthly charge is an employee telling you a tool is worth paying for personally. Treat each finding as product research. (2 min)

### Slide 9: Pass 3: ask, with amnesty
- A 5-question survey: what do you use, for what, with what data
- Explicit amnesty, stated in writing
- Managers ask in team meetings, out loud

**Speaker notes:** Live walkthrough part 3. The technical passes find tools; only people tell you why. Amnesty is non-negotiable, because the moment one person is punished, your visibility ends permanently. Show the sample survey (included in the policy template download). (2 min)

### Slide 10: Govern it. The 5-part acceptable use policy
- 1. Approved tools list, with owners
- 2. Data rules: what may and may never go in, by classification
- 3. Human review: AI output is a draft until a person signs off
- 4. Logging and accounts: company accounts only, access revoked on exit
- 5. The request path: how to get a new tool approved, with a decision deadline

**Speaker notes:** The centerpiece. Walk each part in one sentence. Stress part 5: a policy without a fast request path is just a ban with paperwork, and bans create the problem you started with. A decision deadline (days, not quarters) is what makes the policy real. Template of all five parts is in the follow-up email. (4 min)

### Slide 11: The sanctioned path pattern
- One approved tool with enterprise controls per job to be done
- A stated fast lane for new requests
- Publish the yes list, not just the no list

**Speaker notes:** The pattern that replaces bans. People route around a slow no; nobody routes around a good yes. When the approved tool is genuinely capable, shadow usage collapses on its own, because employees never wanted the risk, they wanted the speed. (2 min)

### Slide 12: Keep the upside
- Governance is the permission slip, not the brake
- ROI follows guardrails; 74% of companies stay stuck in pilots (Grant Thornton)
- Governed AI is provable AI: renewals, questionnaires, board answers

**Speaker notes:** Close the argument. The payoff of the policy is not just risk removed, it is speed gained: projects clear approval because the rules exist. The 74% pilot statistic is the cost of skipping this work. (2 min)

### Slide 13: Your next 30 days
- Week 1: run pass 1 (network, OAuth, SaaS audit)
- Week 2: passes 2 and 3 (paper trail, amnesty survey)
- Weeks 3 and 4: draft the 5-part policy, publish the yes list

**Speaker notes:** Make it a plan, not a takeaway list. One owner, 30 days, tools they already have. Invite them to send questions mid-way; reply-to on the follow-up email is monitored by a person. (2 min)

### Slide 14: Q&A + two things to take with you
- The 5-part acceptable use policy template (link in follow-up email)
- The free AI & Security Readiness Assessment: 10 minutes, scored across 7 pillars
- Q&A: 15 minutes

**Speaker notes:** State the CTA once, plainly, then get to questions. "Two things in your inbox within the hour: the policy template and the assessment link. The assessment shows where shadow AI sits among your other gaps." Then run Q&A; seed with a prepared question if the room is slow. (2 min + 15 Q&A)

## F. Anticipated Q&A (5 questions, prepared answers)

**1. "Why not just block these tools at the firewall?"**
Because blocking does not remove the demand, it removes your visibility. Usage moves to personal phones and personal accounts, where the same data leaves with no logs and no recourse. A ban feels decisive and measures nothing. The sanctioned path works better: one approved, genuinely capable tool per job, plain data rules, and a fast request lane. People route around a slow no. Nobody routes around a good yes.

**2. "We're a 40-person firm. Is this really our problem too?"**
Yes, and arguably more so, because one person's account often touches everything. The exposure scales with data sensitivity, not headcount: client funds, health information, defense contract data. And the outside world already assumes it is your problem: insurance renewals and client security questionnaires now ask about AI use. The good news is the discovery pass is faster at your size. A 40-person firm can finish it in days.

**3. "How do we run discovery without it turning into a witch hunt?"**
Announce amnesty first, in writing, and honor it without exception. The moment one person is disciplined over a survey answer, your visibility ends permanently, and you are back to guessing. Frame the exercise honestly: you are mapping demand to build the approved-tools list. In practice, the people using the most shadow AI become your best allies. They wanted sanctioned tools all along. They just were not offered any.

**4. "What about the AI features inside tools we already approved?"**
That is the fastest-growing category, and the easiest to miss. AI features switch on inside approved SaaS through vendor updates, sometimes with defaults that share data for model training. Fold it into pass 1 of discovery: audit the admin settings of your top ten SaaS tools and record which AI features are on and what each does with your data. Then add a line to vendor reviews: AI feature changes require re-review. Approved vendor does not mean approved AI.

**5. "Our MSP handles our IT. Doesn't this fall to them?"**
Your MSP can help run parts of discovery, and we work with MSPs, not against them. But shadow AI governance is a leadership decision, not a ticket. Someone has to decide which tools get approved, what data rules apply, and which risks you are accepting. That is a different job from keeping systems running. If nobody in your company holds that job, that gap is the finding. It is exactly what fractional security and AI leadership exists to cover.
