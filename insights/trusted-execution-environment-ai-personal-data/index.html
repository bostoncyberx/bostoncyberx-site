The role of Trusted Execution Environments to ensure your company benefits from your data, not the AI company

bcx Research Team · September 13, 2026

On September 8 this year, a trusted execution environment might have prevented one of the messiest attribution fights in modern science. OpenAI announced its agents had solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of seven Clay Millennium Prize Problems. Within days, NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge alleged that OpenAI had raced to publish after learning of their own related progress, built over nearly a year using publicly available models. Buckmaster claimed OpenAI proposed he post first or join a paper that excluded Alpöge because of his Anthropic affiliation.

OpenAI denied accessing the pair's transcripts. Mark Chen denied agents or employees had read them. Buckmaster wrote that OpenAI offered no response on whether its models had been trained on those transcripts. Twenty-five Fields Medalists later signed an open letter on rushed AI math announcements and attribution. The dispute remains unresolved.

The enterprise problem underneath the headlines is older and more common than a math prize: when you send unpublished work, client files, or personal data into a hosted model, you usually cannot prove the operator cannot read it, that it won't be retained, or that it cannot influence later outputs.

"We don't train on your data" is a policy promise.

Trusted execution environment AI architectures exist to close exactly that gap, and the options available to mid-market teams today are more practical than most CISOs realize. Below, you'll find what TEEs actually protect (and what they don't), the products you can evaluate right now, and the checks you should put in place before sending sensitive data to any hosted model.

What is a trusted execution environment?

A trusted execution environment is a hardware-isolated region of a processor where code and data run sealed off from the host operating system, the hypervisor, and the cloud operator. Think of it as a room with no windows: only the code inside can see what's happening, and the hardware itself enforces the boundary.

Your firm already encrypts data at rest and in transit.

That covers storage disks and network pipes. But the moment a model processes your prompt, the data is decrypted in memory. Weights, prompts, and the key-value cache that holds context during inference all sit in plaintext on the host.

The host operator (a cloud provider's admin, a hypervisor, even a rogue insider with root access) can theoretically read that memory.

Policy says they won't. Hardware says nothing.

Why policy promises are not enough

The Navier-Stokes dispute made this gap visible at scale. Whether or not OpenAI accessed the researchers' transcripts, the point is structural: neither Buckmaster nor Alpöge had a hardware-backed way to verify the claim.

They had to take the operator's word for it. So does every enterprise customer sending regulated data to a hosted AI endpoint today.

Enterprises are rapidly adopting generative AI APIs and deploying generative AI-enabled apps in production. That's a lot of firms trusting policy alone to protect data in use.

The problem multiplies when employees spin up model access on their own. If you've dealt with shadow AI from employees using ChatGPT without IT's knowledge, you already know how fast sensitive data can leak into endpoints you don't control. TEEs don't solve shadow AI directly. But they give you a hardware-verifiable option to point your sanctioned workloads toward.

How TEEs solve it

Data in use: the missing layer

You've heard "data at rest" and "data in transit" so many times they blend together. Data in use is the third category, and until recently it was the hardest to protect.

When your prompt enters a model's inference pipeline, it's decrypted. The KV cache holding your conversation context is in the clear. Model weights themselves may be proprietary and exposed.

TEEs encrypt all of that in memory.

The CPU (and increasingly the GPU) handles decryption only inside the enclave. The operator's own infrastructure cannot peek in.

Remote attestation: verify, don't trust

Hardware isolation alone isn't enough if you can't prove it's running. Remote attestation fixes that.

The hardware generates a signed cryptographic quote describing exactly what code and settings are running inside the enclave. Your security team verifies that quote against expected values before sending any data.

You don't take the vendor's word for it. The CPU manufacturer's root of trust signs the attestation, and an independent verifier (like Intel Trust Authority) can validate it. This is the difference between "trust us" and "verify it yourself."

TLS termination inside the enclave

Here's a detail most vendor datasheets gloss over. If TLS terminates at the operator's load balancer, the operator sees your plaintext before it ever reaches the enclave.

For the operator to truly be excluded from the data path, TLS must terminate inside the TEE itself.

Ask about this explicitly when you evaluate a product.

What TEEs do not solve

TEEs are not magic.

They don't solve attribution disputes after the fact. If you already uploaded unpublished research to a non-TEE product last year, a TEE today can't retroactively prove the model never saw it.

They also don't eliminate side-channel risks. Tokenizer-level and memory-access-pattern leakage remain active research concerns.

Bad design can log data outside the enclave, defeating the purpose.

And a TEE protecting inference doesn't help if your fine-tuning pipeline runs on standard compute.

A TEE shrinks the trust boundary. It doesn't eliminate the need for policy and good architecture around it.

Trusted execution environment options you can evaluate now

Several products now offer trusted execution environment AI capabilities you can test against real workloads. Vendor claims are attributed as such.

NEAR AI Cloud

NEAR AI Cloud combines Intel TDX with NVIDIA confidential GPUs. According to NEAR's documentation, prompts are decrypted only inside the enclave, with hardware attestation validated through Intel Trust Authority as an independent verifier.

TLS terminates inside the TEE, which, if implemented as described, keeps the operator out of the plaintext path.

This is one of the more specific public claims about end-to-end enclave isolation for AI inference.

Microsoft Azure confidential VMs with NVIDIA H100

Azure pairs AMD EPYC confidential VMs with NVIDIA H100 GPU confidential compute for both inference and fine-tuning workloads. Microsoft's reference architecture, detailed on learn.microsoft.com, shows how keys are released only to verified workloads through remote attestation, while the rest of the AI stack runs on standard cloud resources.

For mid-market teams already on Azure, this is probably the lowest-friction path to evaluate. You inherit enclave protections with minimal code changes to your existing pipelines.

Phala

Phala runs private AI inference on Intel TDX and NVIDIA confidential computing hardware. The vendor claims client-side quote verification and an OpenAI-compatible API endpoint, which means you could potentially redirect your existing integrations with minimal rework.

We haven't independently validated Phala's attestation chain, and you shouldn't skip that step either.

Apple Private Cloud Compute on Google Cloud

Apple expanded its Private Cloud Compute (PCC) beyond Apple data centers onto Google Cloud infrastructure. The setup uses NVIDIA confidential computing with Intel TDX and Google Titan chips.

Apple's security research documentation claims stateless computation with no privileged runtime access.

This is an unusual architecture: Apple designed PCC so that even Apple's own operators can't see user data during processing. Google's role is infrastructure; the attestation and trust model come from Apple's design.

Meta's WhatsApp Private Processing uses TEEs so that Meta and WhatsApp do not see message contents during processing, as described in engineering.fb.com.

When your workloads need a trusted execution environment

As AI-driven controls become the norm, the infrastructure running those controls matters more.

Should a security agent making access decisions on your behalf run in a verifiable enclave? A compromised hypervisor could otherwise alter how it behaves.

If your firm handles data subject to regulations like Massachusetts 201 CMR 17.00, TEEs add value, and you can't justify avoiding them when confidential compute options exist for your workload class.

Key takeaways

These are the checks you can bring to your next vendor review or board conversation about AI data handling.

  • Prove you protect data in use. Encrypting at rest and in transit is table stakes. If prompts containing personal data or client IP are decrypted on a host the operator controls, your data is exposed during the most sensitive phase: processing.
  • Verify attestation yourself. Remote attestation is only useful if you validate the hardware-signed quote independently. Relying on the vendor to attest their own enclave defeats the purpose.
  • Ask where TLS terminates. If it ends at the operator's load balancer, the operator has plaintext access. TLS should terminate inside the enclave for the trust model to hold.
  • A TEE is not permission to skip policy. Access controls, data classification, and logging outside the enclave still matter. TEEs shrink the trust boundary; they don't replace governance.
  • Choose TEE-backed inference when prompts contain personal, client, or unpublished data. Not every workload needs confidential compute. But when the data is regulated or reputationally sensitive, TEEs are the right control.
  • Frontier consumer AI products operate under a different trust model. Your employees using a consumer chatbot are subject to that product's training and retention policies. Separate consumer convenience from enterprise-grade protection.
  • Side channels remain an open research concern. TEEs reduce exposure dramatically, but tokenizer-level and memory-access-pattern leakage hasn't been fully eliminated. Factor this into your risk calculus for the most sensitive workloads.
